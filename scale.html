Result
  • Free recall of the lens-read item gets weaker, not stronger, with the larger models. With abstention prohibited, the 27B names its reference item in 32.7% of trials, Qwen3.5-122B-A10B in 8.3% and DeepSeek-V4-Flash in 12.1%. All three are above the rate at which they name other trials’ reference items (9.7%, 2.7%, 4.4%). When abstention is not mentioned, the 122B denies having held an item at all in 81% of trials.
  • Recognition falls in the same way but stays above chance. Picking the reference item from four options: strict 44% / 35% / 35%, loose 69% / 45% / 41% (chance 25%). The pairwise slope of answer probability on lens score is positive in every model.
  • The swap behaves the same in all three models. Swapping the A and B coordinates at α = 1 raises the B-vs-A name margin (+0.36, +0.31 and +0.15 nats for the strongest–weakest pairing), but no model names B more than 3% of the time, and multiple-choice recognition does not move at all.
  • The reference item is not special in any of them. Swapping two other items, matched for lens-score gap and edit size, moves their own pair as much as the A–B swap moves A and B. The paired difference is +0.03 / −0.05 nats (27B), +0.01 / −0.02 (122B) and +0.06 / +0.04 (V4-Flash), and no interval excludes zero on the positive side.
  • What this does and does not show. Scale here is confounded with model family, lens quality and precision, and there are only three models. Within these limits, the larger models give no sign of a privileged route from the held item to the report: the swap moves name probabilities through the lens directions generally, as it did in the 27B.

1Models and what changed

Experiment 1 asks whether a model can report an item it was told to hold in mind while copying a sentence, and whether the item the J++ lens reads out during the copy is the one it reports. Here the same 480 situations, the same questions and the same analysis code run on two larger models that have published J++ lenses (koayon/jpp-lenses): Qwen3.5-122B-A10B and DeepSeek-V4-Flash. Each model gets its own stage A (the copy, read with its own lens), its own readout block, its own reference items and its own swap pairings; nothing is carried over from the 27B except the stimuli, the item-name lists and the judge’s labels.

Section 6 lists every change from the 27B run. The main ones: both new models run in bf16 across several GPUs; the readout block is searched up to the same relative depth as the 27B’s; and to fit the $200 budget the swap part (d) runs on a stratified subsample (240 trials for the 122B, 105 for V4-Flash) at α = 1 only.

2(c) Free recall

Each model replies to the question “what item were you keeping in mind?” under three instructions about abstaining. The judge maps each reply to an item name, which is then compared with the reference item: the item the lens scored highest during the copy. The baseline (dashes) scores each reply against other trials’ reference items in the same category, which removes the effect of popular items.

All three models name their reference item more often than the baseline, so the lens readout carries information about what they later say. But the two larger models do so far less often than the 27B. The 122B rarely names anything unless it must: with abstention permitted it answers NONE in 92% of trials, and when abstention is not mentioned 81% of its replies say that it did not, or as an AI could not, keep an item in mind. Even with abstention prohibited, where it always names an item, it names the reference item in only 8.3% of trials. V4-Flash names an item directly in almost every trial, yet it matches the reference only 12% of the time.

The larger models’ replies also sit further from the reference item in probability: in the swap trials, the median log-probability of the reference name as the free-recall reply is −2.4 nats for the 27B, −6.9 for the 122B and −8.6 for V4-Flash. Countries are the easiest category for all three.

3(c) Multiple-choice recognition

Recognition asks the model to pick the item it held from four options. The strict menu takes the top four lens candidates; the loose menu takes the top candidate, two middle ones and the bottom one. Both larger models pick the reference item above chance on both menus, but less often than the 27B. When the model may answer U (none of these), the 122B takes it in most trials (63% strict, 57% loose), in keeping with its free-recall denials; V4-Flash in 8–10%.

The pairwise regression asks the finer question: across all pairs of options, does a higher lens score go with a higher answer probability? It does in every model (strict slopes 3.3, 6.7 and 2.4 nats per score unit). The lens scales differ between models, so the slopes show direction and significance, not a comparable size.

4(d) Swapping J-space coordinates

The swap edits the residual at the readout block on every copied token: the coordinates of the reference item A and a weaker candidate B along their lens directions are exchanged (h += α V(flip(c) − c)). The two controls are a swap of two other candidates C and D, matched to A and B for lens-score gap and edit size, and a random perturbation of the same norm. B is the weakest candidate (strongest–weakest pairing) or the median one (strongest–median). The edit is small, 2.2–3.1% of the residual norm, and the copy stays exact in every edited run.

In every model the swap moves the free-recall name probabilities toward B, by 0.2–0.36 nats in the two Qwen models and 0.12–0.15 nats in V4-Flash. The random perturbation moves nothing. But the swap almost never changes which item is named: under abstention prohibited no model names B in more than 3% of trials. In the 27B the swap lowers the rate of naming A by 2–5 points relative to the controls (significant); in the larger models this drop is not there (0 to 2 points, not significant).

If the model’s report drew on the reference item in a privileged way, swapping A with B should move the report more than swapping two other items matched for lens-score gap and size of edit. A positive difference would mean it does.

The paired difference is the cleanest test of specificity, and it is near zero in all three models. For the strongest–median pairing in the 27B it is even negative: the non-reference pair moves slightly more than A and B. So the swap shows that these lens directions are causally connected to which names the model finds likely, not that the reference item has a special link to the report.

Recognition is untouched in all three models. The change in the B-vs-A margin of the answer letters is under 0.025 nats everywhere, its intervals include zero, and it does not track the change in lens score.

5What the models say

Edited runs that change the free-recall reply are rare: 34 of 954 trial–pairings in the 27B, 6 of 480 in the 122B and 7 of 210 in V4-Flash. In most of them the non-reference swap, and sometimes the random perturbation, produces the same new reply. That fits an edit that nudges the reply as a whole rather than one that replaces A with B. In the 122B, two changes go from naming an item to denying that one was held, and three go the other way.

6Porting choices and deviations

  • Models and lenses. The two largest chat models with published J++ lenses. Gemma-4-31B and OLMo-3-32B were left out: they are the same size as the 27B, and their lenses were fitted on base models.
  • Precision. bf16 sharded over GPUs, the lens authors’ setting (2×H200 for the 122B; 5×H200 for V4-Flash, dequantized from its FP8/FP4 checkpoint, eager attention and experts). The 27B ran in fp32 on one GPU. Edits are computed in fp32 and cast back; coefficients are logged after the cast.
  • DeepSeek’s four residual streams. V4-Flash carries four hyper-connection streams between blocks. Its lens reads their mean and was fitted for a uniform perturbation of all four, so readouts use the mean and the edit adds the same vector to every stream.
  • DeepSeek prompt format. No chat template ships with V4-Flash, so prompts follow the model card’s chat mode with thinking off. Letters A–D and U are single tokens.
  • Readout block. The 27B searched blocks 0–54 of 63. The new models are searched to the same relative depth (0–40 of 47; 0–36 of 42) and the appendix rule picks the block with the most items per position.
  • Item names. The frozen synonym map from the 27B is reused unchanged. Names new to a model were mapped by the same verified-merge step (none for the 122B; mayo → mayonnaise and persia → iran for V4-Flash).
  • Trials. Part (c) runs on all 480 trials for the 122B and on 478 for V4-Flash: in two V4-Flash trials no category item appears in the lens readout, so there is no reference item. Trials with fewer than four candidates get recognition options filled from the category pool (56 trials for V4-Flash).
  • Budget cuts in (d). A stratified random subsample (separate seed): 240 trials for the 122B, 105 for V4-Flash, α = 1 only. The first 39 of the 122B’s trials also ran α = 0.5 before that level was cut to stay within budget. Edited runs ask only the abstention-unspecified and abstention-prohibited questions plus the pair’s recognition question.
  • Denials. The 122B’s denials run past the 16-token reply cap, so the judge never sees them. They are counted as not naming an item, as before, and shown separately by matching how the reply opens (“I cannot”, “I did not”, “As an AI”).
  • Reproduction. Every unedited copy in stage B matches stage A token for token. The lens top 100 does not match exactly in the larger models (top-1 agrees at 83% of positions for the 122B and 90% for V4-Flash; top-100 overlap 90% and 88%), because bf16 numbers differ between a full forward pass and one-token decoding and the mixture-of-experts routing amplifies small differences. The 27B, in fp32, matched at 98% of positions.
  • Specificity test. The paired difference between each swap’s shift of its own pair was added for this page and computed for the 27B too; nothing else in the 27B analysis changed.
